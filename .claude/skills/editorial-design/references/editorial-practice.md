# Editorial practice

How good publications build pages: hierarchy, packaging, furniture, pacing and pictures. "Weak source" means a blog, handout or summary rather than the designer's own words. "Rule of thumb" means nothing was found to cite.

## Principles

1. **One dominant element on every page and spread**, so the eye has somewhere to start. Newspaper practice puts a dominant photo near the top, about twice the size of anything else. (Newspaper design checklists; weak sources)
2. **Readers enter print pages through the dominant picture, then the dominant headline, then captions and teasers, then text.** On screen, headlines drew the eye first more often. (Poynter EyeTrack, as summarised by the Globe and Mail's public editor) In print, put the picture or the headline in charge, not both fighting.
3. **Captions are read** in print and online, so they're a second headline, not a label. (Poynter)
4. **Mix story lengths.** The Guardian's 2005 brief: more short pieces to balance long ones, fewer middling ones. (D&AD Guardian case study)
5. **Build a kit others can use.** Mark Porter treated the paper as "a kind of construction kit" that keeps "a recognisable voice", because non-designers build pages every day. (D&AD) That's the volunteer newsletter exactly.
6. **One wide-ranging family, few sizes.** Porter chose a face with "an extremely wide range of weights" so one family did headline, standfirst and text. (D&AD) Vignelli's two-size limit is his own austerity; most publications need about six roles.
7. **Scale contrast is what makes pages look edited.** twen used "bold contrasts in the scale of pictures and type" (Eye). Turley: charts "start small and scale up according to their merit" (Eye).
8. **White space is a separator, used on purpose.** The Guardian Berliner was praised for "clearly structured" pages with "good use of white space" (Design Observer). Avoid trapped white space: gather it at page edges and between stories. (Newspaper checklists)
9. **Keep a story's parts together** in one rectangle: headline, picture, caption, text. No doglegs. (Newspaper checklists)
10. **Choose one typeface well.** Spiekermann: "It doesn't take any longer to pick the right typeface and try it out than it does to pick the wrong one." A good template with one suitable face gets you most of the way. (Reedsy interview)
11. **Judges reward immediate impact with nothing competing.** SND: "clean, bold and attention-grabbing"; "nothing in the entry that takes away from the impact". SND's criteria: focus, organisation, balance, proportion, contrast, unity, colour, and whether information "can be found quickly". (snd.org) SPD, D&AD and the European Design Awards publish almost nothing on editorial criteria.
12. **Pictures should work as hard as the text.** Porter reversed "write first, find a picture later". (D&AD)

## What particular designers did

**Mark Porter, The Guardian, 2005.** Berliner format, five fairly wide columns replacing eight. Guardian Egyptian (Barnes and Schwartz) after trying hundreds of faces, with weights wide enough for everything. News justified, comment ranged left: alignment as a signal of voice. Critics' warnings from launch week: don't splash a new colour everywhere, don't repeat the same picture slot day after day, don't let teasers and masthead eat the lead. (D&AD; Design Observer)

**Richard Turley, Bloomberg Businessweek, from 2010.** Built on the Guardian's grid thinking: "the grid is there to be broken by graphics, diagrams and pictures". Helvetica for neutrality, "just a typeface, rather than a branding exercise". "It is fine to crop pictures. It's good to crop pictures." Full-bleed only when a picture can carry the page alone. Off-grid pictures, big numbers and display type as occasional "syncopated moments". "A cover should just be surprising." (Eye, "Taking care of business") The lesson: a strict plain base is what lets a few broken moments register.

**Matt Willey, NYT Magazine, Port, Elephant.** "Headlines often don't carry that much information, or aren't as crucial in the same way that the dek is." "Layout is concerned with the combinations of things; the headline, the dek, the image, the white space." He moves things "until something begins to work". He accepts making readers work a little to read a headline; for a community newsletter, legibility comes first. (AIGA Eye on Design)

**Willy Fleckhaus, twen, 1959 to 1971.** A 12-column modular grid with "a lot of white space perfectly arranged", blown-up headlines in Schmalfette Grotesk set tight, tightly cropped or enlarged black-and-white photos, the spread as the unit, cut-outs against squared-up pictures. Commissioned photography for one consistent look. (Readymag; Eye review)

**Alexey Brodovitch, Harper's Bazaar.** Generous white space, off-centre and cropped images, two-page layouts. His early work was busier; he refined towards simplicity. (Weak sources)

**David Hillman, Nova and the Guardian (1988).** Put legs, not a face, on a Nova cover, and it sold. Mocked a rival for "type 15 feet high for no apparent reason". Preferred "less but better". (City of Sound interview)

**Erik Spiekermann.** Redesigned The Economist in 2001; detail of that work wasn't found. *Stop Stealing Sheep* is the readable introduction to type and layout.

## Page furniture

Sizes are relative to body text. They're rules of thumb: no source with measured ratios was found. Test them by eye.

| Element | Size | How |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | Lead 3 to 5×, secondary 1.8 to 2.5×, brief 1.3 to 1.6× | Largest at top of page, smaller down it. Spans the story's columns. Sentence case, ranged left. Never two headlines butting side by side. |
| Standfirst (dek) | 1.2 to 1.4×, often italic or lighter | Directly under the headline. One or two sentences that add what the headline lacks. |
| Byline | Body or smaller | Under the standfirst or at the foot. Plain. |
| Drop cap | 2 to 3 lines | Feature openers only, one per page at most. |
| Crosshead | Body bold, or 1.1× | Every 150 to 250 words in long pieces. More space above than below. |
| Pull quote | 1.5 to 2.5× | On the same page as the quoted text. One per page or spread. Hang the opening mark. (wou.edu magazine layout notes) |
| Caption | 0.8 to 0.9× | Below or beside the picture. Adds who, where, why; never repeats the headline. (Poynter) |
| Photo credit | 0.7 to 0.8× | Close to the picture, one style throughout. |
| Folio and running head | 0.8× | Same place on every page, every issue. |
| Jump line | 0.8× | Avoid jumps entirely in anything under 8 pages. |
| Box or sidebar | Body or 0.9× | A tint or a rule, not both. Only for something that stands apart. |
| Fact block | 0.9× | When, Where, Cost, Contact, always in that order. |
| Contents | Body | Top of page 1 on a short publication. Helps people find what they want. (Diocesan design guide; weak source) |
| Diary or listings | Body or 0.9× | One grid: date, event, time, place. Group by day. Tabular figures. |

## Packaging and hierarchy

- **Rank before designing.** Lead, secondary, brief. One lead per page with the largest headline and the dominant picture, high up.
- **Rectangular modules** of varied width and depth, stacked. Large photos high, varied placement, several entry points. (Newspaper checklists)
- **Second and third entry points:** a pull quote, a fact block, a second picture. (Rule of thumb)
- **Package related items:** one headline over several events on the same day, a shared tint or rule, one picture for the group.
- **Give the lead room.** Don't let teasers and summaries squeeze it. (Design Observer on the Guardian)
- **Pictures face into their story.** (Newspaper checklists)

## Pacing

- **Alternate dense and airy.** Don't run two features that look alike in a row. (Weak source)
- **Think in spreads.** A page and the one beside it are seen together, even if one is an ad.
- **Magazine shape:** quick, voice-led front section; feature well with few ads; shorter pieces and listings at the back.
- **Consistent furniture lets the big elements vary.** Same masthead, running head and folio every issue; change the cover or front-page lead each time.
- **Four-page newsletter:** page 1 opens (masthead, lead, contents); pages 2 and 3 form the spread, one with the feature and big picture, one calmer; page 4 is the back (diary, contacts, ads). Give the diary its own area rather than scattering dates. (Rule of thumb)
- **Ads:** labelled or clearly separate, in their own area, never beside editorial on the same subject. Keep the front page lead and opinion clear of them. (Alberta Magazines code; print tradition)

## Pictures

- **Crop hard when the picture can take it.** (Turley; twen; Brodovitch)
- **One big, the rest smaller.** Equal sizes read as a contact sheet.
- **Full-bleed only for strong pictures.** (Turley)
- **Captions add information.** (Poynter)
- **A weak amateur photo:** crop to the one person or thing that matters, run it small with a strong caption, or give a mixed set one treatment (all black and white) for unity. Never enlarge past its resolution. (Rule of thumb)
- **No picture:** a big headline, standfirst and pull quote as entry points; or a separate feature photo with its own caption.
- **One photographic style** helps a publication hang together. (Fleckhaus)

## Community newsletters

- Left-align body text. Two or three fonts, the same every issue. Text in columns. One alignment edge per page; being off by a little reads as a mistake. Plenty of white space. The most important picture the largest, because people read size as importance. Check how it prints, as paper differs from screen. (Parish and diocesan design guides, Kall Kwik Bury St Edmunds; weak sources)
- Matt paper for text-heavy issues, gloss for picture-heavy ones. (Kall Kwik)

## Amateur mistakes and fixes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Everything centred | Range left; centre one deliberate item at most |
| Too many fonts | One family plus one companion, fixed roles |
| All-caps headings | Sentence case |
| Justified narrow columns with rivers | Ragged right, or wider columns with hyphenation |
| Boxes round everything | White space and one thin rule; box only true sidebars |
| Clip art | Drop it |
| Inconsistent gaps | One spacing unit (the baseline) for everything |
| Ads mixed into editorial | Own area, clearly separate |
| Everything the same size | Rank; one dominant element |
| Equal pictures scattered | One large, smaller supports, facing the story |
| Trapped white space | Gather it at edges and between stories |
| Tiny text so it all fits | Cut copy first; never below the size floor |
| Many colours | One accent with a job to do |
| Dense grey | Crossheads, a pull quote, a picture |
